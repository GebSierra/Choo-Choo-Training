# Reading App Curriculum: Scope & Sequence

Oct 5, 2026 · @TBC Team

## Design principles

This curriculum takes kids from hearing sounds to reading real stories in 10 stages and 54 units, organized around how the brain actually builds reading. It keeps the core phonics ideas of a sound-by-sound progression but regroups them into families, moves high-value skills earlier, and turns most "sight words" into patterns kids can decode.

**From Barbara Oakley's learning research:**

- **Chunking.** Kids build small chunks and link them into bigger ones: sound, then letter, then word, then phrase, then sentence, then story. Related patterns are taught as one family (for example, er, ir and ur all say the same sound), so one chunk unlocks many words.
- **Retrieval practice.** Kids produce the answer (say the sound, read the word, build the spelling) instead of re-watching it. Recognition-only tasks create an illusion of competence.
- **Spaced repetition.** Every learned item comes back on a growing schedule, so practice moves from short-term to long-term memory.
- **Interleaving.** New patterns are first practiced alone, then mixed with look-alike patterns (cap/cape, pin/pen) so kids learn to tell them apart. Because interleaving feels more difficult for kids, keep a balanced approach that leans towards interleaving but lets kids feel like they are successful so they continue practicing.
- **Focused and diffuse modes.** Short sessions (5 to 15 minutes). Sleep consolidates learning, so the app discourages cramming in new hoards of new material every day and encourages shorter but frequent sessions. (ensure the app has a test to determine child placement at the beginning, and leaves room for the child to retake this in the grownups menu)
- **Procedural fluency.** Reading must become automatic. Practice continues past "got it right once" until it is fast and effortless.

**From the science of reading:**

- **Systematic, explicit phonics.** Every sound-spelling is taught directly, in a planned order, from simple to complex.
- **Phonemic awareness, linked to letters.** Kids blend and break apart sounds every day, using letter tiles as soon as the letters are known, because practice with letters roughly doubles the benefit.
- **Orthographic mapping.** Kids connect each sound to its letters, which is how words become instantly recognizable. This is why "sight words" become **heart words** here: decode the regular parts, learn only the tricky part "by heart."
- **Decoding plus encoding.** Every lesson pairs reading a pattern with spelling it.
- **Decodable text.** Early stories use only patterns and heart words already taught, so kids read by sounding out, not guessing from pictures.
- **Language comprehension.** Vocabulary and meaning checks are built into every story, because reading is decoding times understanding (the Simple View of Reading).

## Research Foundations

The strongest, most replicated findings support explicit systematic phonics, phonemic awareness taught with letters, spelling practice, and repeated oral reading with feedback; evidence for decodable books and for reading apps in general is real but weaker. A note on Barbara Oakley's principles: chunking, retrieval and spacing draw on general cognitive-science research. Spacing has direct evidence in young children (above); interleaving and the "focused/diffuse" framing were not verified with young readers in this review, so treat them as design choices to test.

## Rules the App Follows

These guardrails keep the app aligned with the science of reading and away from whole-language and three-cueing habits. Every lesson, story, hint and feedback message must follow them.

1. **No guessing prompts.** Hints never say "look at the picture," "what would make sense," or "what letter does it start with, then guess." The only hint is to sound it out: tap a letter to hear its sound, then blend.
2. **No predictable or patterned books** ("I see a cat. I see a dog.") as reading practice; they train memorizing and guessing. Patterned texts may be read *to* the child for language and enjoyment.
3. **No whole-word memorizing of decodable words.** A word is only taught as a heart word if part of it is truly irregular, and even then the regular letters are sounded out.
4. **Every word in a child-read story is decodable or a taught heart word,** until the planned transition to less-controlled text.
5. **Correct errors immediately and specifically.** Point to the exact letters that were misread, model the correct sounds, and have the child reread the word. Don't just say "try again."
6. **No distracting extras inside reading.** Minimal to no tap-for-surprise hotspots, mini-games or pop-up dictionaries in stories. Rewards come after reading, not during it.
7. **Accuracy before speed.** Timed fluency goals start only after a pattern is read accurately.

## Did You Know? Tips for Parents

These 16 short blurbs turn the research in this document into parent-friendly tips, each short enough for an in-app card or a social post. Each pairs one finding with one thing parents can try at home.

1. **Did you know?** Teaching letter sounds step by step is one of the most tested ideas in education. Decades of studies agree that systematic phonics helps kids learn to read. Choo Choo Training utilizes this research to shape its app.
2. Did you know? Frequent and consistent learning outperforms occasional and long sessions? Find a consistent time when your child can practice reading in the app.
3. &#32;Did you know? Speaking is the foundation of reading. Encourage your kids at home to "put it in a sentence." When they reply with one-word answers like "milk" or "play," ask them to turn it into a full sentence, and model what you want. Say, "I want milk" or "I want to play."
4. Did you know? Our app is built around the model of "Demonstrate and then imitate." Children learn best when they can see or hear it done first. If your child is stuck on a sound first demonstrate saying it, then ask them to imitate it: "You try!"
5. **Did you know?** Strong readers are adept at looking at every letter in a word. When your child gets stuck on a word, try saying "Let's sound it out."&#32;
6. **Did you know?** Kids blend better when sounds are stretched together ("mmmaaat") instead of chopped apart ("m... a... t"). Chopping makes kids forget the first sound by the time they reach the last.
7. **Did you know?** Say /m/ as "mmm," not "muh." Extra "uh" sounds make blending harder: "buh-a-tuh" doesn't sound like "bat." Try: keep stop sounds short and crisp, like a tiny hiccup.
8. **Did you know?** Sound games work about twice as well when letters are involved. Try: say "sun," then have your child find the letter that makes the first sound.
9. **Did you know?** Spelling practice makes kids better readers, not just better spellers. Try: say a simple word like "map" and have your child build it with magnet letters.
10. **Did you know?** Mixing up b and d is a normal stage of learning to read, common until about age 7 or 8. On its own, it isn't a sign of dyslexia.
11. **Did you know?** Letter names can mislead. "W" is called "double-u," so some kids think it says /d/. Try: when reading, use letter sounds ("this says /w/") instead of letter names.
12. **Did you know?** Short e (bed) and short i (big) sound so alike that many kids mix them up. Try: play "pin or pen?" and "bit or bet?" Listening for the difference helps reading too.
13. **Did you know?** Kids often read the first letter and guess the rest. Try the "change one letter" game: sat, sit, sip, tip, tap. It trains kids to look at every letter.
14. **Did you know?** Short, spaced practice beats long sessions. Kids remember more when learning is spread over days. Try: 10 to 15 minutes a day instead of an hour on the weekend.
15. **Did you know?** Rereading the same short story builds reading fluency. Try: read a page aloud first, then let your child read it 3 or 4 times over a few days.
16. **Did you know?** Most "sight words" are only partly tricky. In "said," only the "ai" is unusual. Try: sound out the regular letters together, and learn just the tricky part by heart.
17. **Did you know?** How you correct matters. Point to the exact letter, say its sound, and have your child reread the word. That works better than a general "try again."
18. **Did you know?** Reading is two skills: sounding out words *and* understanding language. Reading aloud to your child builds the second one. Try: read books above their level and chat about new words.
19. **Did you know?** A typical child reads about 60 words a minute at the end of first grade and 100 by the end of second. These are averages, not deadlines; steady progress is what matters.

## The daily lesson loop

Every lesson should be about 5 to 15 minutes, and follow the same general pattern so that kids spend their energy on reading instead of figuring out what to do next. Below are some examples of evidence backed methods we will use.

1. **Sound warm-up (1 min).** Blend spoken sounds into a word ("/m/ /a/ /t/... mat!") and break a word into sounds, then do the same with letter tiles using letters already taught. Sound play without letters is used only in Stage 1.
2. **Quick review (2 min).** Spaced-repetition cards pulled from earlier lessons. The child says the sound or reads the word before seeing or hearing the answer.
3. **New chunk (2 min).** One new sound-spelling, taught explicitly: hear it, see the letter drawn into a picture that starts with that sound (for example, an s shaped like a snake), then say it.  It helps if kids can see an animation or video of someone else's mouth shaping that sound. Only one new idea per lesson.
4. **Blend it together (2 min).** The app models blending ("I do"), then blends with the child ("we do"). We call this "Demonstrate then imitate."
5. **Read it alone (2 min).** The child reads words with the new pattern mixed with review patterns ("you do").
6. **Build it (2 min).** Spelling practice: hear a word, tap or drag the letter tiles to build it. This locks in the sound-to-letter connection.
7. **Read real text (2 to 3 min).** A sentence or mini-story using only taught patterns and heart words. The child reads aloud with immediate, specific correction, then answers one meaning question. Stories are reread on later days (3 to 4 reads total) after hearing a fluent model.
8. **Celebrate and rest.** A reward moment, then the session ends or offers a break. Short sessions leave time for the brain to consolidate.

**Language strand (2 to 3 times a week, separate from the reading loop):** the app reads aloud (or the parent reads aloud) a richer story the child can't yet read alone, teaches 2 to 3 new vocabulary words with their spellings shown, and asks talk-about-it questions. This builds the language-comprehension half of the Simple View of Reading without asking kids to guess at words they can't decode.

**Text progression:** stories the child reads are fully decodable through Stage 5. From Stage 6, up to about 1 in 10 words may use a pattern not yet taught, and the app pre-teaches those words before the story. By Stages 9 and 10, kids read lightly controlled, natural-sounding text, because the evidence for decodable books is strongest only in the earliest phase.

## Mastery, review and pronunciation rules

A child moves to the next unit only after showing both accuracy and speed, and every learned item keeps coming back on a spaced schedule.

**Mastery gate (end of every unit):**

- 90% or better accuracy on the unit's sounds, words and spellings.
- Each sound produced within about 2 seconds, and each word read without stopping to sound out every letter (by the end of the stage).
- One decodable story read with 1 or fewer errors per sentence (when the child attains to the stage of reading)
- These thresholds are explained to the parent in the app. We believe the parents will be the greatest influence in their kids lives, and should have the option to indicate they believe their child is ready for the next unit.
- Not passed: the app adds targeted practice on missed items, then retests. No child moves on with gaps.

**Universal pronunciation rules (apply everywhere in the app's audio):**

- **Stretchy sounds** can be held: /a/, /e/, /i/, /o/, /u/, /m/, /s/, /f/, /n/, /l/, /r/, /v/, /z/, /sh/, /th/. Say them long and smooth: "mmm," not "muh"; "sss," not "suh."
- **Bouncy sounds** are short and clipped: /t/, /p/, /k/, /b/, /d/, /g/, /j/, /ch/. Say "t," not "tuh." Never add an "uh" after a consonant, because "buh-a-tuh" doesn't blend into "bat."
- **Voiced vs. quiet pairs:** some sounds come in pairs made with the same mouth shape, one with the voice box buzzing and one quiet (/v/ and /f/, /z/ and /s/, voiced /th/ and quiet /th/). Kids can check by touching their throat.
- **Blending style:** model blending by stretching continuously ("mmmaaat") rather than chopping ("m... a... t"). This is easiest when words start with a stretchy sound, which is why the first words do.

## Trouble Spots and How the App Handles Them

Most early reading errors come from a short, predictable list: added "uh" sounds, chopped blending, mirror-image letters, look-alike and sound-alike pairs, misleading letter names, short vowels, and consonant clusters. The letter order in Stage 2 and the blending method below are designed around this list.

| Trouble spot | Why it trips kids up | How the app teaches around it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adding "uh" to bouncy sounds ("tuh," "puh") | "Tuh-a-puh" doesn't blend into "tap." Stop sounds can't be stretched, so kids pad them with a vowel | All audio uses clipped stop sounds. Words where the stop comes **last** (at, sat, map) are taught before words where it comes **first** (tap, pit). For a first-position stop, the child says it fast and jumps straight into the vowel: "ta...p" |
| Forgetting the first sound while blending | Saying sounds with pauses ("sss... aaa... nnn") overloads memory; kids taught that way more often forgot the first sound (Gonzalez-Frey & Ehri) | Connected blending only: sounds are stretched and joined without a break ("sssaaannn"); see the blending section below |
| Mirror letters: b, d, p, q | The visual system treats mirror images as the same object; reversals are a normal stage, common until about age 7 or 8, and not a sign of dyslexia on their own | p is taught early and alone; d comes 2 units later; b waits 3 more units; q comes last. Each gets a distinct picture cue and tracing practice with stroke direction. b/d contrast drills start only once both are secure |
| Look-alike letters: m/n, n/u/h, f/t, i/l | Letters with little contrast in shape are harder to learn | Look-alikes are taught several units apart: m (2.1) and n (2.5); n (2.5) and u (2.7) |
| Sound-alike pairs: t/d, p/b, f/v, s/z, k/g, m/n, f/th | The sounds differ only in voicing or in one small mouth movement | The second sound of each pair is taught as the "buzzing partner" of the first, with a touch-your-throat check and minimal-pair drills (tip/dip, fan/van, fin/thin) |
| Short e vs. short i | They sound very close and are commonly confused by beginners | Taught far apart (i in 2.2, e in 2.9), each with a strong picture anchor, then contrasted: pin/pen, bit/bet |
| Letter names that mislead | Kids use letter names to guess sounds. That helps for letters like b and v, but w's name leads kids to /d/, y's name leads to /w/, and h's name has no /h/ in it | The app uses letter **sounds** in all reading tasks; names come later. w, y and h get extra practice, and y is contrasted with w (yet/wet) |
| Vowel letters read with their names | "a" is read as /ay/ because that's the letter's name | Short-vowel picture anchors (a for apple) and consistent use of sounds, not names, during reading |
| Reading the first letter and guessing the rest | Beginners decode the start of a word and stop attending to the middle and end | Daily word chains that change one letter in any position (sat, sit, sip, tip, tap), based on the Word Building intervention |
| Dropping the second consonant in clusters | Kids hear "st" as one unit and read or spell "stop" as "sop" | Clusters are built with separate letter tiles, and chains add a sound: sap, snap; tick, stick |
| Dropping n or m before a final stop | In words like "sand," the n melts into the vowel, so kids write and read "sad" | Non-nasal ending blends (st, ft) come first; nasal ones (nd, mp, nt) come after, with contrast pairs: sad/sand, cap/camp |
| Still-developing speech sounds (/r/, /l/, /th/) | Many young children can't yet say these sounds clearly, which can be mistaken for a reading error | Speech recognition accepts close approximations of these sounds, and reading mistakes are judged on the word, not on articulation |

## How We Teach Blending

Blending is taught as one smooth, connected sound stream, never as chopped sounds, and words get harder in a planned order. In a kindergarten study, children taught connected blending ("sssaaannn") decoded new words, including words starting with stop sounds, more accurately than children taught to pause between sounds.

**The blending progression:**

1. **Hear it first (Stage 1).** The app or parent says a word slowly and smoothly ("mmmaaat"); the child picks the matching picture.
2. **Two-sound words with stretchy sounds.** am, at, it. The child slides a finger under the letters while saying one unbroken sound.
3. **Three-sound words starting with a stretchy sound.** Sam, sat, mat, sit, map. The stop sound, if any, comes last.
4. **Words starting with a stop sound.** tap, pit, pat. The child says the stop quickly and attaches it straight to the vowel ("ta... p"), never "tuh."
5. **Word chains every lesson.** Change one letter at a time in the first, middle or last position (sat, sit, sip, tip, tap, map). This forces attention to every letter, especially vowels.
6. **Fade the support.** Letters light up one by one as the child sounds, then in time the child is encouraged to whisper-read, then reads the whole word on the first try. The goal is instant reading of familiar patterns.
7. **Blends and longer words.** Two consonants stay joined with no "uh" between them ("sssnap," not "suh-nap"). Two-syllable words are split, each part is read, then the parts are glued: nap + kin.

**When a child gets stuck:** the app first replays the connected model. If the child is still stuck, it builds the word cumulatively from the start (s, sa, sat) so the child holds only one growing chunk. This cumulative step is a widely used support, though it has been tested less directly than connected blending.

**Error-correction script (used everywhere**** ****a****n****d**** ****t****a****u****g****h****t**** ****to paren****ts****):**

1. Highlight the exact letter that was misread.
2. Say its sound: "This says /i/."
3. Have the child blend the whole word again.
4. Have the child reread the whole sentence, so the corrected word is practiced in context.
5. Bring the missed word or sound back in the next review session.

## Grade-Level Map

The curriculum is grouped into 4 reading levels matched to U.S. grade expectations: **Pre-K** (Stage 1 and the first 6 letters), **Kindergarten** (the rest of Stage 2 and the first part of Stage 3), **Grade 1** (Stages 3 to 7) and **Grade 2** (late Stage 7 to Stage 10). Levels are matched to the Common Core foundational reading standards and the Head Start preschool outcomes, with fluency targets from national oral-reading norms.

| Level | Typical age | Stages and units | What the standards expect by the end of the level | End-of-level benchmark in the app |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Pre-K** | 4 to 5 | Stage 1; Units 2.1 to 2.3 (m, a, s, i, t, p) | Head Start (by 60 months): says the first sound of a spoken word; names at least half the letters and gives the sounds of many | Hears and says first sounds; knows the sounds of m, a, s, i, t, p; blends 2- and 3-sound words; reads "Sam sat" |
| **Kindergarten** | 5 to 6 | Units 2.4 to 2.11; early heart words; Unit 3.1 | Common Core K: the main sound for every consonant; short and long sounds for the 5 vowels; high-frequency words by sight (the, of, to, you, she, my, is, are, do, does); telling apart words that differ by one letter; reading beginning-reader texts | Reads short-vowel words and decodable sentences; knows all K heart words; reads a short decodable story with 1 or fewer errors per sentence |
| **Grade 1** | 6 to 7 | Units 3.2 to 7.4: digraphs, blends, endings, 2-syllable words, open vowels, silent e, common vowel teams | Common Core 1: digraphs; regular one-syllable words; silent e and common vowel teams; counting syllables by vowel sounds; 2-syllable words; inflectional endings; grade-level irregular words | Oral reading of about 60 words correct per minute on an unpracticed passage (the national 50th percentile for spring of Grade 1; 29 in winter) |
| **Grade 2** | 7 to 8 | Units 7.5 to 10.6: more vowel teams, bossy R, sliding vowels, tricky patterns, prefixes and suffixes | Common Core 2: long vs. short vowels; more vowel teams; 2-syllable words with long vowels; common prefixes and suffixes; inconsistent but common spellings; irregular words | About 100 words correct per minute (50th percentile, spring of Grade 2; 84 in winter) |

**How the levels work in the app:**

- **Levels are labels, not gates.** Kids move by mastery, not by age or grade. A 4-year-old can be at the Kindergarten level and a struggling 7-year-old at the Pre-K level; the app never shows a child a "below grade level" message.
- **Placement check.** A short check at sign-up (letter sounds, blending, a few words at each level) places the child at the right unit instead of making every child start at Stage 1.
- **Parent view.** Parents see the level label, the current unit, and how the child compares with the grade benchmark.
- **Fluency targets start in Grade 1.** National oral-reading norms begin in the middle of Grade 1, so earlier levels use accuracy benchmarks only. A child reading 10 or more words per minute below the benchmark gets extra fluency practice.
- **Rereading for meaning is allowed, guessing is not.** From Grade 1, the standards expect kids to use context to *confirm or self-correct* a word they've decoded. The app asks "Does that make sense? Read it again" only **after** the child has sounded a word out, never instead of sounding it out.
- **Beyond Grade 2.** Grade 3 work (longer multisyllable words and more prefixes and suffixes) is not yet in this curriculum and is a natural next expansion.

Kids learn that words are made of sounds and that we read left to right, all before letters appear. It lasts only 2 to 3 sessions, because sound practice without letters pays off less than practice with letters; after Stage 1, sound play uses letter tiles.

## Stage 2: First Words · Pre-K (2.1 to 2.3) and Kindergarten

Kids learn all 5 short vowels and the single consonants, reading real words from the first unit and growing from 2-word sentences to their first story. The first 6 letters are fixed: **m, a, s, i, t, p**. The rest are ordered to keep known trouble spots apart: mirror letters (p, d, b) are spread over 5 units, look-alikes (m/n, n/u) are separated, short e comes last and far from short i, and letters whose names mislead (h, w, y) get extra practice. At a typical pace of about 3 new letter-sounds a week, Stage 2 takes roughly 8 weeks.

| 2.6 | /k/ spelled c and k, /h/ | cat, cap, cot, can, kit, kid, hat, hit, hot, him |  | Same sound, two letters: usually c before a, o, u and k before i, e. /h/ is just a breath, not "huh." Its name ("aitch") has no /h/ in it, so h gets extra practice |
| 2.7 | /u/ as in "fun" (short u), /g/, /l/ | up, cup, sun, fun, mud, hug, gum, dig, log, lip, lid |  | u is a flipped n, so it comes 2 units after n, with contrast drills. /g/ is the "hard" sound as in "gap" (soft g comes in Stage 10) and the buzzing partner of /k/. /l/ is stretchy. **4-word sentences**: "Tom had a cup." |
| 2.8 | /r/, /b/ | rat, rip, run, rug, bat, bib, big, bud, cab, rob |  | b comes 3 units after d and 5 after p, with a different picture cue and tracing practice showing stroke direction. b/d contrast drills begin only after both are secure: bad/dad, bud/dud. /r/ is stretchy; many young kids are still learning to say it, so speech recognition accepts close approximations. **First story** unlocks |
| 2.9 | /e/ as in "bed" (short e) | bed, red, pen, net, leg, ten, get, hen | he, me, be, go, no, so | Taught alone and last because short e and short i sound so alike. Contrast drills are essential: pin/pen, bit/bet, pat/pet |
| 2.10 | /j/, /w/, /v/ | jam, job, jug, jet, wig, web, wet, van, vet | want, was, we | w's name ("double-u") doesn't contain /w/ and can lead kids to say /d/, so w gets extra practice. /v/ is the buzzing partner of /f/: fan/van |
| 2.11 | /y/ as in "yellow", /z/, /x/, /qu/ | yes, yet, yum, zip, zap, fox, six, box, quit, quiz | my, you, of, said, do, does, are | y's name starts with /w/, so kids often say /w/ for y; contrast yet/wet. /z/ is the buzzing partner of /s/. /x/ is two sounds, /k/ + /s/. q almost always comes with u and says /kw/. Stage ends with a mixed review of all short vowels |

The phrases "I want" and "I want to" become available once "want" and "to" are taught, giving kids early sentences about themselves.

## Stage 3: Digraphs · Late Kindergarten to Grade 1

Kids learn that two letters can team up to make one brand-new sound. The app shows digraph letters as a single linked tile so kids see them as one chunk.

| Unit | New sounds | Example words | Heart words | Teaching and pronunciation notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3.1 | /sh/ as in "mash", /ch/ as in "chip" | ship, shop, fish, mash, chip, chop, much | she | /sh/ is stretchy ("shhh"); /ch/ is a quick sneeze sound, not "chuh" |
| 3.2 | /th/ voiced as in "that" and /th/ unvoiced as in "thin" | **Voiced:** that, this, them, then. **Unvoiced:** thin, thing, thank, bath, math | they | Both are taught together as a contrasting pair. Tongue peeks out between the teeth for both; touch your throat: "that" buzzes, "thin" doesn't. Quiet /th/ is often mixed up with /f/, so it's drilled against it: fin/thin. Once th is known, "this" is fully decodable |
| 3.3 | ck as in "back"; doubled ff, ll, ss, zz as in "puff," "bell," "miss," "buzz"; /wh/ | back, sick, duck, lock, puff, bell, miss, buzz, when, which | what, where | One spelling rule, one chunk: ck and doubled ff, ll, ss, zz usually come right after a short vowel at the end of a one-syllable word, and the two letters make one sound. wh says /w/ in most American speech. In "what," the a says /u/ |
| 3.4 | Ending ng as in "long", "sing", "rang" and ending nk as in "bank" | rang, sing, long, hung, bank, pink, honk, junk |  | Taught as sound chunks: -ang, -ing, -ong, -ung and -ank, -ink, -onk, -unk. The vowel sounds a little different before ng and nk, so kids learn the whole chunk |
| 3.5 | Mixed review | Interleaved digraph and short-vowel words |  | Contrast sets: ship/chip, sack/shack, thin/tin. Review extra-difficult sounds from Stages 2 and 3 |

## Stage 4: Blends · Grade 1

Kids learn that blends are two sounds side by side, each still heard, unlike digraphs which make one new sound. Blends are grouped into families so one rule covers many words.

| Unit | New patterns | Example words | Heart words | Teaching and pronunciation notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4.1 | Ending blends: st as in "lost", ft as in "soft", then nd as in "and", mp as in "lamp", nt | lost, best, soft, gift, and, hand, lamp, jump, tent |  | Ending blends come before beginning blends because kids can sound out the start of the word normally. Non-nasal blends (st, ft) come first. In nd, mp and nt, the n or m melts into the vowel, so kids often read or spell "sand" as "sad"; the app highlights that letter and drills contrast pairs: sad/sand, cap/camp, bet/bent |
| 4.2 | s-blends: st as in "stick", sp as in "spot", sk as in "skip", sn as in "snack", sw as in "swim", sm, sl | stick, stop, spot, spin, skip, skin, snack, snap, swim, smash, slip |  | s is stretchy, so hold it and slide into the next sound: "ssstop." No "uh" between: "sss-top," not "suh-top." Kids often drop the second consonant ("sop" for "stop"), so word chains add the missing sound: sap, snap; top, stop |
| 4.3 | l-blends: bl as in "block", cl as in "clam", fl as in "flap", pl as in "plum", gl | block, black, clam, clap, flap, flag, plum, plan, glad |  | Glide the first sound straight into /l/: "block," not "buh-lock" |
| 4.4 | r-blends: br as in "brush", tr as in "trip", cr as in "crab", dr as in "drop", fr as in "frog", gr | brush, brag, trip, trap, crab, crib, drop, drum, frog, from, grin |  | Glide into /r/: "trip," not "tuh-rip." tr often sounds close to "chr" and dr close to "jr"; that's normal speech |
| 4.5 | Mixed review: blends at both ends | stamp, crust, blend, frost, plant |  | Longest short-vowel words yet (5 sounds). Interleave blends with digraphs: crash, flesh, think |

## Stage 5: Word Endings and Bigger Words · Grade 1

Kids learn that word endings change meaning and that long words are just short words glued together. This is moved earlier than usual because it multiplies the number of words kids can read using only short vowels.

| Unit | New patterns | Example words | Heart words | Teaching and pronunciation notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5.1 | Plural and action -s, and -es | cats, maps, dogs, bugs, runs, boxes, wishes |  | The s says /s/ after quiet sounds (cats) and /z/ after buzzing sounds (dogs). -es adds a whole beat after s, x, sh, ch: box-es |
| 5.2 | Ending -ing | jumping, fishing, singing, resting |  | Kids find the base word first, then add the ending. Teach as one chunk: /ing/ |
| 5.3 | Ending -ed, three sounds | **/t/:** jumped, wished. **/d/:** filled, spilled. **/ed/:** landed, rested |  | The spelling is always -ed but the sound changes: /t/ after quiet sounds, /d/ after buzzing sounds, and a whole extra beat /ed/ after t or d. Revisit later with "cooked," "played" and "shouted" once those vowels are taught |
| 5.4 | Compound words and 2-syllable short-vowel words | sunset, catfish, bathtub, napkin, rabbit, picnic |  | Split between the two consonants, read each chunk, then glue: nap + kin. Clap the beats first |
| 5.5 | Mixed review | Interleaved endings and 2-syllable words |  | Story length grows to several short pages |

## Stage 6: Long Vowels, Part 1 (Open Vowels and Silent E) · Grade 1

Kids learn the first two ways a vowel "says its name": standing alone at the end of a word, and getting help from a silent e. Every silent-e pattern is taught with its short-vowel partner so kids learn to tell them apart.

| Unit | New patterns | Example words | Heart words | Teaching and pronunciation notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6.1 | Open vowels: ending long e as in "we" and long o as in "go"; ending y says long i | we, me, he, be, she, go, no, so, hi, my, by, fly | bye | When a vowel is last, it usually says its name. This is the bridge to long vowels. "Bye" adds a silent e |
| 6.2 | Silent e: a\_e as in "bake" | bake, make, cape, game, lane, shade |  | The e is silent, jumps back over one consonant and makes the vowel say its name. Contrast pairs: cap/cape, man/mane |
| 6.3 | Silent e: i\_e as in "bike" and "ride"; o\_e as in "hole" and "rope" | bike, ride, hike, time, hole, rope, home, stone |  | Contrast pairs: kit/kite, pin/pine, hop/hope, rod/rode |
| 6.4 | Silent e: u\_e as in "tube" or "flute" and as in "cute"; e\_e as in "eve" and "here" | cube, cute, use, tube, flute, tune, eve, theme, Pete |  | u\_e has two sounds: /yoo/ as in "cute" and /oo/ as in "tube." Try one, then the other, and pick the real word. e\_e is rare. In "here," the r changes the vowel a little |
| 6.5 | Mixed review: all silent-e patterns | hike, rope, make, tune, eve, plus contrast pairs | have, give, live | Exception lesson: English words don't end in v, so these words get an e that doesn't change the vowel. Learn "have" by heart |

## Stage 7: Long Vowels, Part 2 (Vowel Teams) · Grade 1 (7.1 to 7.4) and Grade 2

Vowel teams are grouped by the sound they make, so kids learn "long a can be spelled ai, ay or a\_e" as one connected chunk instead of scattered facts. Within each unit, one spelling is taught at a time, then the spellings are mixed.

| Unit | Sound family | Example words | Heart words | Teaching and pronunciation notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7.1 | **Long a:** ai as in "rain", ay as in "hay", ey as in "hey" | rain, train, paint, hay, play, day, hey, today, away | again | Position rule: ai sits in the middle of a word, ay sits at the end. ey says long a in a few words (hey, they). "Today" and "away" are now decodable. In "again," the ai says short e |
| 7.2 | **Long e:** ee as in "keep", ea as in "read" and "sea", ending y as in "happy", ie as in "cookie" | keep, tree, see, read, sea, eat, happy, funny, cookie |  | Ending y says long e in longer words (happy) but long i in short words (my). ea sometimes says short e (bread); teach that later as a second sound |
| 7.3 | **Long o:** oa as in "coat", ow as in "snow", oe as in "toe" | coat, road, boat, snow, blow, grow, toe, goes |  | Position rule: oa in the middle, ow at the end |
| 7.4 | **Long i:** ie as in "pie", igh as in "high" | pie, tie, lie, high, night, light, bright |  | igh is a 3-letter team: the gh is silent. Review y as in "my" |
| 7.5 | **oo and long u:** oo as in "food" and oo as in "good", ue as in "blue", ew as in "flew" and "news" | food, moon, good, book, cooked, blue, glue, flew, news, few |  | oo has two sounds: long as in "food," short as in "good." Try both and pick the real word. "Hooray" is now decodable |

Kids learn that an r after a vowel takes control and changes the vowel's sound. The biggest shortcut is teaching er, ir and ur together, since all three make the same sound.

| 8.2 | or as in "for", ore as in "more" | for, fork, corn, horse, short, more, store |  | "Horse" is now decodable: or + a silent e |
| 8.3 | er as in "her", ir as in "bird", ur as in "turn" | her, fern, bird, dirt, girl, turn, burn, hurt | were, worth | Three spellings, one sound. Learn the sound once, then learn which spelling each word uses. After w, or often says this sound too: worth, work, word |

Kids learn vowel sounds where the mouth slides from one shape to another during the sound. Each unit pairs the two spellings of one sound with a simple position rule.

| Unit | New patterns | Example words | Heart words | Teaching and pronunciation notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 9.1 | ou as in "out", ow as in "how" | out, shout, shouted, house, mouse, our, how, cow, down |  | Position rule: ou in the middle, ow often at the end. ow has two sounds (snow, how), so try both and pick the real word. "House," "mouse" and "our" are now decodable |

Kids learn the less common spellings and see that most "weird" words actually belong to small families. Grouping them this way turns dozens of memorized words into a handful of patterns.

## What Changed From the Source, and What to Check

The source's core ideas are all here (short vowels first, digraphs before blends, silent e, vowel teams, bossy R, sentence-length milestones, every pronunciation note), but the structure, grouping, sequence, example words and teaching notes are rebuilt.

**Biggest changes and why:**

- **Activity numbers replaced** with 10 stages and 54 mastery-gated units, so progress depends on learning, not on finishing a fixed count.
- **Heart words moved earlier.** "I," "the," "to" and "want" arrive in Stage 2 instead of hundreds of activities in, so kids can read meaningful sentences sooner.
- **Sight words reclassified.** 17 former sight words are taught as decodable patterns, and the rest mark only the tricky part.
- **Patterns grouped into families:** blends by type (s, l, r), both th sounds as one contrasting pair, er/ir/ur as one sound, vowel teams by the sound they make.
- **Word endings and 2-syllable words moved earlier**, which multiplies readable words without new vowel sounds.
- **Look-alikes separated and contrasted:** b is taught well after d, short e well after short i, and every silent-e pattern is drilled against its short-vowel partner.
- **Added for completeness:** x, z, qu, wh, aw, soft c, the words said/you/of/should/give/live, and daily spelling practice.
- **Two source items reinterpreted:** "/ae/ as in fae" became ey as in "hey," since "fae" isn't a word kids will meet. The cut-off note "not the sound in..." on u\_e became the two u\_e sounds (cute vs. tube).

**Book unlocks:** the source unlocked six published picture books (*Green Eggs and Ham*, *Hop on Pop*, *Today I Will Fly!*, *The Eye Book*, *Are You My Mother?*, *Waiting Is Not Easy!*). Those are copyrighted titles that would need licensing to include in an app. This curriculum instead unlocks a library of original decodable stories at the end of each stage; you could still recommend real books as "read with a grown-up" suggestions.
