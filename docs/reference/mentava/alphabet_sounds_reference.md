# Mentava Alphabet Sounds: Reference Summary

Purpose: a plain-language summary of the book "Alphabet Sounds" by Mentava, written so another AI can use it as source material for a reading app.

## 0. About this summary

* Source: a 75-page landscape PDF. Author and Mentava founder: Niels.
* I read the text of all 75 pages. I looked at four pages as images (pages 4, 17, 50, 63) to confirm the layout.
* "The book says" marks the author's own claims. "My note" marks something I observed or inferred. Keep them apart.
* Numbers in the book (for example "99% of letters are lowercase", "under 100 hours", the age ranges) are the author's claims. I have not verified them.
* The word lists below are taken from the word labels under the illustrations. I picked 5 to 7 per sound. The book gives about 10 per sound.

### How the book writes sounds (notation)

* A sound with a trailing hyphen, like "b-", means a short, clipped sound with no vowel added.
* A sound written with repeated letters, like "fff" or "mmm", means the sound can be held.
* My note: the book never names these two groups. This is only how it writes them.
  * Clipped (hyphen): b, c/k, d, g, h, j, l, p, t
  * Held (repeated letters): f, m, n, r, s, v, w, z, sh ("shhh"), short e ("ehhh")
  * Written as two sounds: q ("kw"), x ("ks")

## 1. The book's teaching point of view

The book's main claim: most alphabet books, and many professional reading programs, teach reading in ways that confuse children. Small errors in how sounds are said add up when the child starts blending. The author says getting the details right makes reading "seem easy" and getting them wrong makes it "nearly impossible".

The rules. The book does not number them. The order and grouping are mine:

1. **Teach sounds, not letters.** English has 26 letters but 44 sounds (the book's count). Many sounds take two letters (sh, oi, oo). Typical alphabet books skip them. They even leave out both "th" sounds.
2. **Practice each sound alone before using it in words.** The book says reading words gets hard if sounds are not taught and practiced in isolation.
3. **Teach lowercase letters first.** The book says 99% of what children read is lowercase.
4. **Do not teach letter names** ("ay, bee, see"). Call letters by their sounds ("aaa, mmm, sss"). Reason: "cat" is not said "see-ay-tee". Using names and sounds together confuses children when they start sounding out words.
5. **Say each consonant without adding "uh".** Not "buh", "fuh", "tuh". The appendix calls this the most common mistake. Say it as if a spoken word were sliced into pieces.
6. **Blend sounds with no pause between them.** "fun" is said "fffuuunnn", not "fuh-uh-nuh". The book says even top YouTube reading teachers get this wrong by telling children to pause.
7. **Never change a sound during blending.** Example from the book: one top program starts with "as", but the "s" in "as" turns into a "z". That is an irregular word and a poor first blending word.
8. **Be consistent, every time.** The book's words: say letter sounds correctly every single time, write letters correctly every single time. It also says children learn to write one shape of "a" and "g" but books show another. (My note: the "a" on the a page is the single-story round "a". I did not check "g".)
9. **Stretch the target sound inside the word.** The book's example: "This is aww! Can you say aww? This is an OOOctopus. Can you say octopus? This is a bAAAll. This is a yAWn." The target sound can be at the start, middle, or end of the word. The picture pages mix all three positions.
10. **Hearing comes before saying.** The appendix separates recognition (hearing a sound) from recall (knowing which letter makes it). It says recognition is required first. This skill is called phonemic awareness.
11. **The child's own pronunciation does not need to be right.** The book says to enjoy the baby talk. A child can learn to hear differences before being able to say them.
12. **Keep it fun and shared.** Treat it as time with a parent. Progress will be uneven.
13. **One letter can have many sounds, and one sound many spellings.** Book examples: "a" in cat, can, cane, car, call, many, father. The "i" sound in I, eye, pie, mine, my, high. "oo" in foot and food is ambiguous. The book's answer is to teach each sound as its own unit, then show the different spellings in the word examples.
14. **Use a neutral American accent.** The book skips small differences such as merry/marry/mary and paw/lot. It notes that some Southern accents say "pin" and "pen" the same. It says learners with other accents or first languages may need extra practice with subtle pairs.
15. **Use several kinds of feedback when teaching blending.** The book says Mentava's software uses real-time audio, visual, and touch feedback so children blend correctly.

### Readiness (appendix)

* Four skills are needed to read: (1) hearing and telling apart spoken sounds, (2) memorizing letter and sound pairs, (3) left to right order, (4) blending sounds into a word.
* The book says a child can learn letter sounds before being ready for left to right order and blending. Parents may see early success with sounds, then a struggle when blending starts. The book says nothing is wrong; the child needs more time.
* Readiness test for left to right order: emoji pairs "dogfish" and "fishdog". Guide the child's finger left to right and say which is which. Spend a minute or two. It is a check on development, not a skill to drill. The book says most children can do it around age 3.5.
* Ages when children tend to be able to say sounds (the book's approximate figures):
  * Age 2: vowels, and p, b, m, d, n, h, t
  * Age 3: k, g, w, ng, f, y
  * Age 4: l, j, ch, s, v, sh, z
  * Last to come: r, voiced th, unvoiced th, zh

## 2. How the book divides the sounds

The page order is: single-letter sounds (alphabetical, with short vowels mixed in), then two-letter consonant sounds, then vowel pairs, then r-controlled vowels, then one special case, then the comparison pages.

| Group | Sounds (page order) | Pages |
|---|---|---|
| A. Single letters and short vowels | a, b, c/k, d, e, f, g, h, i, j, l, m, n, o, p, q, r, s, t, u, v, w, wh, x, y, z | 4 to 29 |
| B. Other consonant sounds | sh, ch, th (voiced), th (unvoiced), ng, zh | 30 to 35 |
| C. Long vowels and vowel pairs | ae, ee, ie, oe, ue/oo (moon), oo (book), ue (you), ow, oi | 36 to 44 |
| D. R-controlled vowels | ar, or, er/ir/ur | 45 to 47 |
| E. Special case | raised a | 48 |
| F. Sound comparisons | 23 comparison pages, pairs that are easy to confuse | 49 to 72 |

My notes on the structure:

* The order is alphabetical inside group A. The book does not say it is a teaching order.
* Short vowels are called the "default sound" for a, e, i, o, u. Each has an "as in" word: apple, egg, igloo, ox, up.
* Long vowels are labeled as sounds, not letters: ae, ee, ie, oe. The word examples then show several spellings for each.
* There is one page for "c,k" together. There is no separate k page.
* I found no page for soft c, soft g, silent letters, or the vowel sound in "saw/law" (only a comparison page).
* The book says English has 44 sounds. I did not try to match its pages to that number.

## 5. Things to check before using this as source data

1. **Two pages are titled "e vs. i".** The first has long "ee" words against short "i" words (heat/hit). The second has short "e" words against short "i" words (bet/bit). They are different contrasts with the same title.
2. **"ooh vs. u" uses "u" for the "book" sound** (pull, full, foot), not the "up" sound. This differs from how "u" is used elsewhere in the book.
3. **"aw vs. o": I am not certain what "o" means there.** The words suggest long o (low, sew, boat, coat). The word "bot" does not clearly fit either side. Verify against the page.
4. **The "o" page includes "yawn", "dog", "frog".** The book says it ignores the paw/lot difference. This matches the intro, where "yawn" is used as the "aww" example. The book has no separate "aw" sound page.
5. **Some example words do not show the sound cleanly.**
   * "choir" is on the q page. It has the "kw" sound, but is not spelled with q.
   * "x-ray" is on the x page. The x there is said by its letter name.
   * "narwhal" appears on both the n page and the wh page.
   * "tank" and "drink" are on the ng page. They have ng followed by a k sound.
   * "mansion" is on the sh page. The sh sound is spelled "si".
   * "desert" and "dessert" are both on the z page.
6. **The "y" page gives two hints that may not match.** It says "yee", and also to say "yo" and stop before "oh".
7. **The o page says "as in ox", but the picture of the ox is on the x page, not the o page.**
8. **Typo in the book:** "umbrellla" (three l's) on the u page.
9. **The PDF text layer has stray labels.** "Bat" shows up on many pages where there is no bat. "Moose" and "Law" also appear in odd places. I ignored these.
10. **The intro claims specific facts** (children as young as 3 reaching second grade reading level in under 100 hours; 99% lowercase). I have no verified source for these.
